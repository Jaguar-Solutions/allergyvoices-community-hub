## What changed

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has been consulting with stakeholders
on **food allergen thresholds** — the amounts of allergenic protein below
which the vast majority of allergic individuals would not experience a
reaction. Threshold policy could reshape how "may contain" statements,
cross-contact protocols, and risk assessment work in the U.S.

This isn't an immediate rule change. It's an early-stage conversation, but
one that could meaningfully affect how families navigate labels and
restaurants over the next several years.

## What food allergen thresholds are (and aren't)

Thresholds are **population-level reference points**:

- **ED01** — the eliciting dose that affects 1% of allergic individuals
- **ED05** — the dose that affects 5% of allergic individuals

These thresholds don't replace personal diagnosis or your child's specific
reaction history. They're tools that help regulators and food
manufacturers decide *when* trace allergens require disclosure and *how*
to design cross-contact controls. The most-sensitive individuals
(below ED01) remain the priority in safety decisions.

## Why it matters to families

If U.S. thresholds are adopted, three real-world effects are most
plausible:

- **Labeling clarity.** Currently, "may contain peanut" appears across
  products with very different actual risk levels. Thresholds could
  trigger consistent rules: a "may contain" statement only when residue
  exceeds a defined threshold. Some products you currently avoid out of
  caution might be safe; others you'd been giving a pass to might warrant
  more care.
- **Restaurant standards.** Threshold-aware cross-contact protocols
  could give restaurants and bakeries clearer guidance on cleaning
  practices, shared equipment, and acceptable risk levels.
- **Hidden allergens.** Trace amounts that aren't currently required to
  be declared might trigger disclosure under threshold rules.

## Where other countries stand

The U.S. is behind on this. Several food safety bodies have been
working on threshold-based approaches for over a decade:

### European Union

The **European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)** has published reference
doses for several priority allergens and applies risk-based
allergen-control guidance. The EU also recognizes the **VITAL** system
(see below).

### Australia & New Zealand

**Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ)** developed **VITAL**
(Voluntary Incidental Trace Allergen Labelling) — the most mature
threshold-based labeling framework in the world. Many manufacturers
internationally use VITAL even when not required, because it gives them
a defensible framework for "may contain" decisions.

### Canada

**Health Canada** has consulted on thresholds and precautionary labeling
but hasn't formally adopted threshold values. Their approach has tended
to track international research and best practices.

## What families should know

- **Don't change your daily practices yet.** No U.S. rule has been
  finalized. Your current avoidance plan, action plan, and emergency
  preparedness all remain in place.
- **The most-sensitive individuals must remain the priority.** Threshold
  policy is meant to clarify labels for *most* allergic people; it's not
  a license for casual exposure.
- **"May contain" remains complicated.** Until thresholds are codified,
  these statements stay voluntary and inconsistent. Talk to your
  allergist about how strictly to interpret them.
- **Watch for FDA listening sessions.** The FDA periodically holds
  public meetings and accepts written comments. Families have a real
  opportunity to be heard during these windows.
