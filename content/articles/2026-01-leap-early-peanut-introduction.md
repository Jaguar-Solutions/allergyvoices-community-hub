## What changed

For decades, parents were told to **delay** introducing peanut to babies —
sometimes until ages 2 or 3 — in the hope of preventing peanut allergy.
The 2015 **LEAP trial** (Learning Early About Peanut Allergy) flipped that
advice on its head.

LEAP enrolled over 600 infants ages 4-11 months with severe eczema, egg
allergy, or both, and randomized them to either consume peanut-containing
food regularly or avoid peanut entirely until age 5. The result:

- **Children who consumed peanut regularly had an 81% lower rate of peanut
  allergy by age 5** compared to the avoidance group.

The 2024 **LEAP-Trio** follow-up confirmed something critical: that
protection persists into adolescence, even after children resume normal
eating patterns. Early introduction doesn't just delay the allergy — it
appears to prevent it.

## Who this affects

The original LEAP trial focused on **high-risk infants** (severe eczema,
egg allergy, or both). The 2017 NIAID guidelines (still current) recommend
three approaches based on infant risk:

| Risk profile | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| **Severe eczema and/or egg allergy** | Allergy testing first, then introduce peanut as early as 4-6 months, ideally under supervision |
| **Mild-to-moderate eczema** | Introduce peanut around 6 months at home |
| **No eczema, no food allergy** | Introduce peanut freely with other solid foods |

The bottom line for most healthy infants: **introduce peanut early and
regularly, around 4-6 months of age**, alongside other solid foods.

## What families should know

- **"Early" means 4-6 months**, not 12 months. Waiting until the first
  birthday is the old advice and is now considered higher-risk for
  developing allergy.
- **High-risk infants need a plan with their pediatrician or allergist**,
  not casual exposure. Some allergists recommend a supervised first
  feeding for high-risk babies; others test for IgE first.
- **Whole peanuts are a choking hazard** for infants. Use peanut butter
  thinned with water/breast milk/formula, or peanut puffs that dissolve
  in saliva.
- **Once introduced, keep going.** LEAP showed sustained, regular
  consumption (~2 grams of peanut protein, 3+ times per week). Sporadic
  exposure isn't enough.
- **The same principles likely apply to egg.** Early introduction of egg
  appears to reduce egg allergy risk too. Other allergens are less
  studied but the consensus is "don't artificially delay."

## What this doesn't mean

- It doesn't mean every baby will avoid peanut allergy. Some babies still
  develop allergy despite early introduction. The risk is reduced, not
  eliminated.
- It doesn't mean older children should suddenly start eating their
  allergens. If your child is already diagnosed with peanut allergy,
  follow your allergist's plan — usually avoidance plus epinephrine,
  with OIT or omalizumab as treatment options.
- It doesn't apply if your child has had a clear allergic reaction to
  peanut. That's an allergist conversation.

## Editorial note

This is a major, family-impacting finding. If your baby is in the 4-11
month window and you haven't introduced peanut yet, this is a worth-a-call
conversation with your pediatrician this week.
