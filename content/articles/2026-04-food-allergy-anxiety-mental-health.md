## What changed

The clinical food allergy community has, over the last decade, moved
from treating anxiety as a side issue to treating it as a **core part of
food allergy management**. The 2022 AAAAI practice parameter on food
allergy explicitly recommends screening for psychosocial issues in every
patient.

Why the shift? Because the research is clear that:

- **Up to 1 in 3** patients with food allergy report clinically
  significant anxiety related to their condition
- **Parents and caregivers** experience anxiety at similar or higher
  rates than the patient
- **Untreated anxiety affects outcomes** — worse quality of life, fewer
  social activities, sometimes worse adherence to safety protocols (or,
  paradoxically, hypervigilance that makes life smaller)

## What food allergy anxiety looks like

Anxiety can show up in many forms. Some common patterns:

**In children:**

- Avoiding social events, parties, or sleepovers because of food
- Refusing to eat foods they previously enjoyed (because of fear of
  hidden allergens, even when there's no risk)
- Stomach pain, nausea, or panicked feelings around meals
- Repeatedly checking labels, asking adults to verify, asking the same
  question multiple times
- Sleep difficulties, especially before days with social food events

**In teens:**

- Withdrawal from peer activities involving food (eating out, parties)
- Avoiding dating because of social complexity
- Skipping meals at school
- Reluctance to carry auto-injectors visibly

**In parents and caregivers:**

- Difficulty leaving the child with anyone — family, sitters, school
- Sleep disturbances, especially after a reaction
- Feeling unable to enjoy meals or social events
- Constant scanning for allergens in shared spaces

## How to tell anxiety from a real reaction

This is one of the trickier parts. Some symptoms overlap:

| Symptom | Could be anxiety | Could be reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lump in throat / can't swallow | ✓ | ✓ |
| Stomach pain / nausea | ✓ | ✓ |
| Rapid heartbeat | ✓ | ✓ |
| Lightheadedness | ✓ | ✓ |
| Hives, swelling, breathing changes | usually not | ✓ |
| Symptoms peak quickly then fade with calming/distraction | ✓ | usually not |
| Symptoms get worse over 5-15 minutes | usually not | ✓ |

When in doubt, **treat it as a reaction**. Use epinephrine if any
serious symptoms are present. Anxiety itself never gets worse from a
dose of epinephrine; an untreated reaction does.

## What helps

The well-supported approaches:

**Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** with a therapist who knows food
allergy is the most effective treatment. Sessions typically focus on:

- Distinguishing anxious thoughts from realistic risk assessments
- Building exposure ladders for situations being avoided (eating at a
  restaurant, going to a party)
- Practical coping strategies for high-anxiety moments

**A clear, written emergency action plan** that the patient and family
both understand. Anxiety is often worse when "what to do if" is
ambiguous. A signed plan with explicit thresholds reduces uncertainty.

**Confidence-building experiences** under appropriate supervision —
practice with the auto-injector trainer, role-playing the dining-out
script, attending an allergy-friendly camp.

**Peer support** from other allergy families. Online and in-person
groups consistently rank as helpful.

**Treatment of the allergy itself** sometimes helps anxiety. For some
families, OIT or omalizumab dramatically reduces threshold-related
fear. For others, it doesn't change anxiety much. Worth discussing with
your allergist.

## When to seek professional help

Reasonable triggers for a referral:

- Avoidance is making life smaller (skipping school events, social
  withdrawal)
- Multiple symptoms before every meal that aren't reactions
- After a severe reaction or near-miss, ongoing fear that doesn't ease
  in 4-6 weeks
- Sleep difficulties, school refusal, or noticeable mood change
- A parent or caregiver is struggling to let the child engage in
  age-appropriate activities

Your allergist's office is usually the right starting point for a
referral. FARE maintains a directory of mental health professionals
with food allergy expertise.

## What families should know

- **It's common.** You're not alone, and this isn't a sign of weakness
  or bad parenting.
- **It's treatable.** CBT for food allergy anxiety has good evidence and
  outcomes.
- **It's distinct from "in your head."** The anxiety is real, the
  symptoms are real, and the treatment is real.
- **Catching it early is easier.** Patterns that go on for a year are
  harder to unwind than patterns caught in the first few months.
- **Parents need support too.** Treating parental anxiety often
  improves the child's anxiety as a side effect.

## Editorial note

If you read one section of this article, read "what helps." Anxiety
that's gone untreated for a long time can shape a child's relationship
with food and social life. Treatment works, and it's worth the call.
