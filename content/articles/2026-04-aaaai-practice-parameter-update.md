## What changed

The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI) and its
sister organizations periodically update the **Food Allergy Practice
Parameter** — the consensus document that guides how U.S. allergists
manage food allergy. The most recent updates over the past few years
have shifted the field meaningfully.

The headline themes:

- **Early introduction** of allergens (peanut especially, egg
  increasingly) is the standard for prevention in infancy
- **Active treatment options** (OIT, omalizumab) deserve a real
  conversation in every appropriate patient, not just the most severe
- **Mental health and quality of life** are now part of standard food
  allergy care, not optional extras
- **Updated diagnostic approaches** for component testing and oral food
  challenges

## Who this affects

Anyone managing a food allergy in the U.S. The practice parameter is
written for clinicians, but it shapes the questions a well-informed
patient or parent should bring to their visits.

## What's actually in the updated guidance

### Diagnosis

- **Component testing** (IgE testing for specific protein components,
  not just whole-food extracts) is recommended in many situations,
  particularly for peanut, hazelnut, and egg
- **Oral food challenges** remain the gold standard for confirming or
  ruling out allergy when blood/skin tests are ambiguous
- The parameter is explicit that **a positive blood test alone, without
  a clinical history of reaction, doesn't equal allergy**. Lots of
  unnecessary food avoidance starts with this confusion.

### Prevention

- **Early peanut introduction** for high-risk and average-risk infants
  alike (per NIAID guidelines)
- **Early introduction of egg** is increasingly recommended
- **No clear evidence** for delaying any other allergen in the absence
  of a known reaction

### Treatment

The updated parameter takes a much more affirmative stance on **active
treatment** than older versions:

- **Oral immunotherapy (OIT)** is recommended as a treatment option for
  appropriate patients, not just an experimental approach
- **Omalizumab (Xolair)** since its 2024 FDA approval for multi-food
  allergy
- **Combination protocols** (omalizumab + OIT) at experienced centers
- **Strict avoidance + epinephrine** remains a fully appropriate choice
  for patients who don't want active treatment

The point is that families should have a real conversation about
options, not just be told "avoid the food and carry epinephrine."

### Mental health and quality of life

The parameter now explicitly recommends:

- **Screening for food allergy anxiety** at routine visits
- **Referral to mental health professionals** with food allergy
  experience when indicated
- Recognition that **parental anxiety** affects family outcomes and
  deserves attention

### Action plans and emergency preparedness

- Every patient should have a **written emergency action plan**, signed
  and current
- **Two epinephrine auto-injectors** prescribed and updated regularly
- **Annual review** of the action plan and reaction history
- Updated guidance on **flu vaccines for egg-allergic patients** (most
  vaccines now safe for most patients)

## What families should know

- **Bring the practice parameter to your visits** — or at least, bring
  the questions it raises. "Have we talked about OIT?" and "Is our
  action plan up to date?" are simple questions a current parameter
  expects every patient to address.
- **The parameter changes faster than most older clinical norms.** If
  your allergist hasn't mentioned OIT or omalizumab, it's reasonable to
  ask why — not as a confrontation, just as part of an informed
  decision.
- **The parameter is for the U.S.** International guidelines (EAACI,
  Australasian) overlap heavily but have some differences worth knowing
  about if you travel or move.
- **The parameter is updated every several years.** What's standard in
  2026 wasn't standard in 2018. Long-time patients sometimes haven't
  had their care updated to reflect what's now considered best
  practice.

## Editorial note

If your last allergist visit felt like a maintenance check-up rather
than a real conversation about your options, this is the article that
suggests asking for more. The current guidance is clear that food
allergy management has more options than strict avoidance — and that
mental health is part of the picture, not separate from it.
