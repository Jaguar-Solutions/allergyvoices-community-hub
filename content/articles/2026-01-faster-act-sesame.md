## What changed

The Food Allergy Safety, Treatment, Education, and Research Act — the
**FASTER Act** — was signed into law in April 2021. Its core change took
effect on **January 1, 2023**: sesame became the 9th major U.S. food
allergen, joining milk, egg, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat,
and soy under the FALCPA labeling rules.

What that means in practice:

- Packaged foods sold in the U.S. must now declare **"sesame"** in plain
  language — either in the ingredient list or in a "Contains: sesame"
  line at the end.
- Before 2023, sesame could appear under names like *tahini*, *til*,
  *gingelly*, or *benne* with no warning to allergic consumers.

## Who this affects

The FDA estimates **more than 1 million Americans** have sesame allergy,
and prevalence has been rising in children. Sesame ranks as the 9th most
common food allergen behind the original Top-8 in pediatric studies.

Reactions can be severe; sesame is a notable cause of anaphylaxis in
children, especially those with multiple food allergies.

## The unintended side effect

Within months of the FASTER Act taking effect, an unexpected pattern
emerged: **some bakeries began intentionally adding sesame flour to
products that had previously been sesame-free**. Why?

The new law made it easier (and cheaper) for bakeries to declare sesame
on the label than to clean their lines well enough to keep sesame out
of products that didn't traditionally contain it.

This means a few real-world consequences:

- **Recheck older trusted brands.** A bagel, bread, or breadstick you
  bought reliably before January 2023 may have been reformulated to
  include sesame.
- **The "everything bagel" problem.** Many bakeries now add a small
  amount of sesame to all their products, not just sesame-topped ones.
- **Read every label, every time.** A reformulation isn't always
  flagged; the ingredient line just changes.

The FDA has acknowledged this is happening. As of 2025-2026, advocacy
groups continue to push for stronger language around when adding an
allergen is acceptable.

## What families should know

- **The good news outweighs the bad.** Even with the bakery loophole,
  sesame allergy is far easier to manage now than before 2023.
- **The names to recognize:** sesame, tahini, til, gingelly, benne, sim
  sim, gomashio. Any of these mean sesame.
- **Highly refined sesame oil** is sometimes tolerated by sesame-allergic
  patients (the protein is largely removed in refining). Cold-pressed
  or unrefined oil is not. Confirm with your allergist before
  introducing.
- **"May contain sesame"** statements are voluntary; some bakeries use
  them, others don't. Don't rely on their absence.
- **Cross-contact** in restaurants, especially Middle Eastern, Asian,
  and Mediterranean, is common.

## Editorial note

The FASTER Act is exactly the kind of regulatory change that helps
families on a daily basis. The bakery loophole is a real frustration,
but it's a footnote on a much bigger win.
