## What changed

In January 2017, NIH's National Institute of Allergy and Infectious
Diseases (NIAID) published the **Addendum Guidelines for the Prevention
of Peanut Allergy in the United States**. The guidelines translated the
LEAP trial's research findings into specific clinical recommendations
based on each infant's risk profile.

These guidelines are still the U.S. standard. They've been endorsed by
the American Academy of Pediatrics, the AAAAI, and most major allergy
organizations.

## The three categories

NIAID grouped infants into three risk categories, each with a specific
recommendation:

### Category 1: Severe eczema, egg allergy, or both

These infants are at the **highest risk** of developing peanut allergy.
The recommendation:

- **Allergy testing first** (skin prick test or peanut-specific IgE blood
  test), ideally between 4-6 months of age
- **Introduce peanut as early as 4-6 months**, after testing
- For infants with very large skin test reactions or high IgE, **first
  feeding should happen in a clinical setting** (allergist's office)
- For others, supervised at-home introduction is appropriate

### Category 2: Mild-to-moderate eczema

These infants are at moderate risk. The recommendation:

- **Introduce peanut around 6 months of age** at home
- No testing required first
- Continue regular peanut consumption (3+ times per week)

### Category 3: No eczema, no food allergy

This is the **majority of infants**. The recommendation:

- **Introduce peanut freely** along with other solid foods, around the
  age you're introducing solids generally (typically 6 months)
- No special precautions or testing needed
- Continue regular peanut consumption to maintain tolerance

## How to actually do it

Whole peanuts are a choking hazard for infants. Practical options:

- **Smooth peanut butter thinned with water, breast milk, or formula**
  (2 teaspoons stirred into ~2 tablespoons of warm water) &mdash; a common
  starting amount is 2 grams of peanut protein
- **Peanut puffs** that dissolve easily in saliva (Bamba is a common one
  used in research; many U.S. brands also work)
- **Peanut powder** mixed into infant cereal or yogurt

The key targets: **about 2 grams of peanut protein, at least 3 times per
week**, sustained over many months. That's roughly:

- 2 teaspoons of smooth peanut butter, OR
- ~17 peanut puffs, OR
- 2 teaspoons of peanut powder

## Why "regular" matters

LEAP didn't just show that early introduction works &mdash; it showed that
**sustained, regular consumption** is what builds and maintains
tolerance. Sporadic exposure doesn't have the same protective effect.

This is why the guideline emphasizes "3+ times per week, every week, for
many months." Once tolerance is established, occasional missed weeks are
fine, but you don't want to drop peanut from the diet entirely until at
least age 5.

## What families should know

- **Most babies are Category 3.** No special steps required &mdash; just
  introduce peanut along with everything else.
- **High-risk babies should have a plan.** If your baby has bad eczema or
  is already egg-allergic, talk to a pediatrician or allergist before
  introducing peanut.
- **The first feeding is the riskiest one.** Watch for hives, vomiting,
  or breathing changes for at least 30 minutes afterward. Most reactions
  happen within that window.
- **Don't introduce peanut for the first time at daycare or with a
  babysitter.** First feedings happen with the parents present.
- **If you missed the early window, ask your pediatrician.** Late
  introduction isn't useless, but the protective effect is strongest
  in the 4-11 month window.
