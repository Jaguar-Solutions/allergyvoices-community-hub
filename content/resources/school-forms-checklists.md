## The forms (and what they're for)

Different schools call them different things. Three you may encounter:

- **504 plan** — a federal civil-rights protection for students whose
  disability (food allergy qualifies) substantially limits a major life
  activity. It's the document that legally requires accommodations.
- **Individualized Health Plan (IHP)** — nursing-led, written by the
  school nurse. Covers day-to-day care: where auto-injectors are stored,
  who administers, what symptoms trigger what response.
- **Emergency Action Plan** — the one-pager from your allergist with
  symptoms and dosing. The IHP and 504 reference this document.

You may have all three, or just an IHP, depending on your district.
Ask the school nurse what's standard at your school.

## What to ask for

- **Auto-injector storage** that's accessible, not locked away. Best
  practice is one set with the school nurse and one in the classroom
  with the teacher (especially for younger students).
- **Trained staff** — not just the nurse. Cafeteria, recess monitors,
  and teachers who can administer epinephrine. Many states allow trained
  unlicensed school personnel to give it under standing orders.
- **Cafeteria coordination** — know what your child can and can't eat,
  and how the kitchen tracks ingredients.
- **Field trip planning** — auto-injectors travel with the trip. Write
  it into the plan.
- **Birthday and classroom celebrations** — some schools have moved away
  from food-based celebrations entirely. Others have a known
  "non-food-celebration" option for allergic students.
- **Substitute teachers** — the IHP needs to be visible in the lesson
  plans so subs see it.

## Start-of-year checklist

Two to three weeks before school starts:

- [ ] Get a fresh emergency action plan signed by your allergist
- [ ] Refill auto-injectors if they expire during the school year
- [ ] Photo of each auto-injector and the action plan saved on your phone
- [ ] Schedule a meeting with the school nurse
- [ ] Bring two auto-injectors (school + classroom or backup)
- [ ] Review or update the 504 / IHP
- [ ] Introduce yourself to the new teacher, share the action plan
- [ ] Confirm the cafeteria has your child's allergy on file
- [ ] Brief the bus driver if your child rides the bus
- [ ] Talk to your child about the plan in age-appropriate terms

## What to teach your child

- Where the auto-injectors live in the school
- How to ask for help fast (no embarrassment, this is a safety thing)
- "I have a food allergy. I need to call my parent before I eat this."
- That it's OK to ask the nurse to look at any food they're not sure about

## When something goes wrong

Schools sometimes get things wrong. The most common situations:

- A teacher hands out food without checking
- A substitute doesn't know about the allergy
- An auto-injector is locked in an inaccessible office

Document what happened in writing (email is great), share with the school
nurse and principal, and update the plan to prevent recurrence. Most
issues are honest mistakes that can be fixed with clearer communication.

## Older students

By middle and high school, most students carry their own auto-injectors.
Talk about this transition with your child and your allergist:

- Self-carry rights vary by state but are widely supported
- Your child needs to know exactly when to use it (not just have it on them)
- The school still keeps a backup set with the nurse for the first few years
