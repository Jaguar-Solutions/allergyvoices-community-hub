## Before you go

### Connect with disability services

Most colleges treat food allergies as a disability under the ADA, which
means you can request **accommodations** &mdash; the equivalent of a 504 plan
in K-12, but college-flavored. Common ones:

- **Single room** or specific roommate match (especially helpful for
  contact-sensitive allergies)
- **Kitchenette in the dorm** so you can prepare some meals
- **Meal-plan modifications** (waivers, partial plans, off-campus food
  stipend)
- **Priority access** to allergen-friendly dining stations
- **Documented dining-hall accommodations** (custom meal prep, ingredient
  disclosure on demand)

The earlier you start this conversation the better &mdash; ideally before
you commit to a school.

### Meet with the dining director

Once you're enrolled, schedule a real meeting with the head of dining
services or their allergy specialist (most large universities have one).
Ask:

- How are dining hall ingredients tracked? Can students see ingredient
  lists for every dish?
- Is there a dedicated allergen-friendly station? Is it actually
  separated, or just a labeled section?
- Who do I talk to about a specific dish? Can I email a chef directly?
- What's the protocol when a student has a reaction?
- Are there "safe brand" partnerships for packaged options?

The good schools answer these crisply. The not-so-good ones get vague.

### Bring backup food for move-in

Move-in day is chaos. Dining services may not be running normally yet,
and you'll be hungry. Pack a box of:

- Safe shelf-stable snacks
- Microwaveable meals (if you have a microwave)
- Bottled water
- Paper plates and disposable utensils

Enough for 3-5 days while you figure out the dining hall.

## Living in the dorm

### Roommate conversation

Have a real conversation in the first week. Tell your roommate:

- What you're allergic to
- What that means for shared spaces (no peanut butter on the desk, etc.)
- Where your auto-injectors live in the room
- What to do if something happens

Most roommates step up if you're clear. If they don't, you can request
a different placement &mdash; lean on disability services.

### Dorm kitchen rules

If you have access to a shared kitchen, the rules of cross-contact apply:

- **Your own pans, knives, and cutting boards.** Color-code them if
  you're sharing a space.
- **Your own dish sponge.** Sponges harbor protein residue.
- **Wipe down counters before you cook.** Soap and water, not just a
  wipe.
- **Your own labeled fridge shelf.** A bin keeps your food separated
  from a roommate's.

### Always carry

- **Two auto-injectors.** All the time. Backpack, weekend bag, gym bag,
  date night. Always two.
- **Action plan** photo on your phone.
- **Insurance card.**
- **Allergist's number** saved in your phone.

## Dining hall navigation

- **Ingredient cards** &mdash; most dining halls now post an ingredient list
  for every dish. If yours doesn't, ask the manager why and request it.
- **Allergen-friendly stations** &mdash; check whether your school has a
  dedicated station. Stations vary in quality from "labeled section
  with cross-contact" to "fully separate kitchen with dedicated staff."
- **Dish-by-dish questions** &mdash; ask about prep, shared utensils, oils
  used for cooking.
- **The chef relationship** &mdash; introduce yourself. The dining hall is
  almost like a small restaurant; chefs who know you and your allergies
  will look out for you.

## Eating off-campus

The strategies from the [dining-out
guide](/resources/dining-out-script) apply:

- Lead with the script
- Talk to the manager or chef, not just the server
- Ask about cross-contact in shared fryers, woks, grills
- Have a backup plan if a place doesn't feel right

College towns often have a few well-known allergy-friendly restaurants.
Older students at the campus food allergy group (most schools have one)
will know which they are.

## Parties and social events

This is where the social calculus gets harder. Most parties involve some
combination of:

- Pizza ordered for a crowd
- Beer (usually wheat-based; problematic for wheat or some other
  allergies)
- Late-night snacks of unknown origin
- Friends drinking and not paying full attention

Strategies that work:

- **Eat before.** Nothing replaces a meal you cooked yourself.
- **Bring your own.** A safe drink, a safe snack &mdash; nobody minds.
- **Tell at least one sober friend** about your allergy and where your
  auto-injector is. Designate them as your contact if something happens.
- **Have a soft exit plan.** If a place feels unsafe, leave.

## Greek life and clubs

If you're considering joining a fraternity, sorority, or other group with
shared food and travel:

- Talk to the chapter ahead of time. The good ones will accommodate
  meals at events.
- Confirm what travel and overnight events involve food.
- Some chapters are better than others &mdash; talk to current allergic members
  before joining.

## When something happens

- Use your auto-injector at the first sign of anaphylaxis.
- Call 911 or campus security (which can dispatch EMS).
- After EMS arrives, call your parent and your allergist.
- Document what happened &mdash; what you ate, where, what symptoms started
  when. This helps your allergist and is useful if you need to file a
  complaint with the dining hall.

## End-of-year debrief

Once a year, sit down (with a parent or with disability services) and
review:

- What worked
- What didn't
- What needs to change next year (different dorm, different meal plan,
  different accommodations)

The first semester is the hardest. By second year you've found your
people, your safe restaurants, and your rhythm.
