## The three pieces of an emergency plan

1. **A written emergency action plan** signed by your allergist. One page,
   two-column format, with symptoms on one side and what-to-do on the
   other.
2. **Two epinephrine auto-injectors**, in date, kept together.
3. **People who know the plan.** Family members, school nurses, daycare,
   the babysitter, anyone who feeds your child.

Without all three, the plan isn't really a plan.

## Where to put the action plan

- **One copy on the fridge** at home. Anyone in the kitchen can see it.
- **One copy in the bag** that travels with the auto-injectors.
- **One copy at school** with the nurse. Update it at the start of every
  school year.
- **A photo on each parent's phone.** When you're in line at urgent care
  with a stressed kid, pulling up a photo is faster than digging through
  a binder.

## When to use epinephrine

This is the question your allergist's plan answers in writing. The general
guidance most plans use:

- **Use epinephrine immediately** for: trouble breathing, throat tightening,
  swelling of lips/tongue/throat, repeated vomiting, sudden severe hives all
  over the body, drop in blood pressure, dizziness or fainting, or any
  combination of symptoms across two body systems (skin + GI, etc.) after
  a known or suspected exposure.
- **Don't wait** to see if it gets worse. Epinephrine is much more
  effective when given early, and the side effects are mild and short.
- **Call 911 after** giving epinephrine, even if symptoms improve.

If you're not sure whether to give it — **give it.** A dose of
epinephrine is far less risky than waiting through anaphylaxis.

## What happens after epinephrine

- Symptoms usually improve within minutes.
- A second auto-injector may be needed if symptoms don't improve or if
  they return (this is why you carry two).
- **Always go to the hospital** after epinephrine, even if your child
  feels fine. Some reactions return 1-4 hours after the first wave
  ("biphasic reaction"); the ER will observe for that window.

## The calm script

Practice saying these words before you ever need them:

> "My child has a known peanut [milk/egg/etc.] allergy. We just gave
> EpiPen [auto-injector brand] in the [thigh, side]. We're calling 911."

Saying it out loud once or twice in a calm moment makes it easier to find
the words in a stressful one.

## Annual review

Sit down with your allergist once a year (or after any reaction) and
update:

- The action plan, if symptoms or thresholds have changed
- Auto-injector dose if your child has grown (kid → adult dose)
- Expiration dates on auto-injectors and antihistamines
- Anyone new who needs to know the plan (new teacher, new caregiver, new
  babysitter)

## What's not on the plan

Antihistamines (Benadryl, Zyrtec, Allegra) are **not** a substitute for
epinephrine. They can help with mild skin or GI symptoms but they don't
treat the airway or blood-pressure parts of anaphylaxis. They're a
supportive tool, not the emergency tool.

If your plan only includes antihistamines for mild reactions, that's
intentional — just make sure you and everyone caring for your child
knows where the line is.
