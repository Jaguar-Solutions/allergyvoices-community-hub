## The rules of two

- **Always two auto-injectors.** They go together. One isn't a backup, it's
  a second dose for when the first one needs reinforcement (about 20% of
  anaphylaxis cases need a second dose).
- **Always with you.** Auto-injectors at home don't help on the playground
  or at a restaurant. The bag goes where the kid goes.
- **Always in date.** Mark expiration dates on a calendar reminder one
  month early. Refill before it expires.

## Storage

- **Room temperature.** Roughly 68-77°F (20-25°C). Brief excursions outside
  this range are usually fine.
- **Not in a hot car.** Direct sun in a closed car can hit 130°F+ in
  minutes. Heat damages epinephrine.
- **Not in a freezing pocket.** Cold won't ruin it instantly but repeated
  freezing is bad. In winter coats, an inside pocket near body heat is
  better than an outside pocket.
- **Not in a refrigerator.** Some families think this preserves it — it
  doesn't, and cold-stored epinephrine takes longer to work.

## How to use the trainer

Every auto-injector brand ships with a trainer device that has no needle
and no medication. Practice once on a pillow:

1. Take the trainer out of the case.
2. Remove the safety cap (different per brand — learn yours).
3. Press firmly into the outer thigh. Through clothing is fine.
4. Hold for the brand-specific count (varies, usually 2-10 seconds).
5. Remove and gently massage the injection site.
6. Call 911.

Practicing once means your hands know the motion before stress takes over.

## Generic vs brand-name

Generic epinephrine auto-injectors work the same as brand names. The
**device** (how you operate it) is what differs — trainer practice for
your specific device matters more than which brand you have.

If your prescription changes brands at refill, take 30 seconds with the
new trainer.

## After use

- **Call 911.** Always. Even if symptoms improve, even if your child seems
  totally fine 5 minutes later. Biphasic reactions (a second wave 1-4
  hours later) are a known risk.
- **Hand the used auto-injector to EMS.** They'll dispose of it safely
  and confirm the dose given.
- **Replace it.** A used auto-injector is gone. Call the prescriber that
  same day for a new prescription.

## Travel

- **TSA / airline rules** allow epinephrine auto-injectors in
  carry-on regardless of liquid limits. Bring them in their original
  pharmacy-labeled package; don't pack in checked bags (cargo holds get
  cold and inaccessible).
- **International travel:** carry your prescription label and a doctor's
  letter explaining the medication, especially for countries that scrutinize
  syringes/needles.
- **Long flights:** keep auto-injectors in the seat pocket or on you, not
  the overhead bin. If something happens, you have seconds, not minutes.

## When you might need three or more

Some allergists prescribe more than two if:

- You travel often or live far from emergency care
- Your child is at residential camp or boarding school
- A previous reaction needed multiple doses

Ask if this fits your situation.
