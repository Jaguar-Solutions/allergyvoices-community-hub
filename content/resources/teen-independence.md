## The slow handover

Most parents don't go from "managing every meal" to "handing over the
auto-injectors" overnight, and most teens don't suddenly want full
control. The handover usually happens piece by piece over a couple of
years.

A reasonable progression:

- **Middle school:** carry your own auto-injectors with parental oversight.
  Read labels at the grocery store with a parent. Order your own food at
  restaurants while parents listen.
- **Early high school:** order alone at restaurants, talk to the manager
  yourself. Manage your own school accommodations with the nurse. Pack
  your own lunches.
- **Late high school:** travel with friends (with a backup plan). Drive
  yourself to the allergist. Handle your own prescription refills.
- **Pre-college:** make decisions about which schools work logistically,
  meet with disability services, plan dining-hall strategy.

There's no universal timeline. Some teens are ready earlier, some later.
The point is the direction.

## Carrying your own epinephrine

By high school, most teens carry their own auto-injectors. A few practical
things:

- **Always two.** No exceptions. They go where you go.
- **Where they live:** backpack is fine, but not buried in the bottom —
  somewhere fast to reach. A purse or jacket pocket is fine. Definitely
  not in your locker.
- **Self-carry rights** — in most states, schools must let you carry
  your own epinephrine. Your school should have a self-carry agreement on
  file. If they push back, your allergist can write a letter.
- **At parties or sleepovers,** you carry. Don't hand them to a friend.

## Talking to friends

Friends mostly want to do the right thing — they just don't know what
that is. Tell them:

- "I have a serious allergy to [X]. If I have a reaction, here's what
  it looks like, and here's what to do."
- Show them your auto-injector. Show them where it lives in your bag.
- Tell them where you keep your phone and who to call.
- Ask them to learn how to use the trainer (it takes 30 seconds).

Most friends step up. The ones who don't get serious about it — you can
notice and adjust without making a big deal out of it.

## Dating

This is going to come up. A few things that have worked for other people:

- **Mention it early.** Not as a confession, just as part of getting to
  know each other ("oh, by the way, I have a peanut allergy — if we ever
  share food, that matters").
- **Kissing and food allergies.** This is a real thing — reactions can
  happen from kissing someone who's recently eaten your allergen. The
  rule of thumb: if your partner just ate it, brushing teeth + waiting a
  few hours is the cautious path. Your allergist can give you a more
  specific answer.
- **Restaurants on dates.** You handle the menu the same way you would
  alone. The right partner respects this. If they don't, they're not
  the right partner.

## Restaurants and social events

Use the same script your parent uses (see the [dining-out
guide](/resources/dining-out-script)). The first few times solo it
feels awkward. After a couple of months it's automatic.

For parties and gatherings:

- **Eat before** if you're not sure what'll be there.
- **Bring something to share** that you know is safe (gives you a default
  option).
- **Watch out for "allergy-friendly" claims** that aren't backed up. Your
  judgment is better than someone else's "I'm pretty sure it's fine."

## Mental health

Living with a food allergy can mean low-level vigilance every meal. That
adds up. It's normal to feel:

- Anxious about restaurants
- Frustrated when friends don't take it seriously
- Embarrassed about asking questions in public
- Tired of always being "the allergic one"

These feelings are real. If they get heavy or interfere with your life,
talk to a parent, a counselor, or a therapist who knows about food
allergies (FARE has a list). You're not being dramatic; allergy anxiety
is a known and treatable thing.

## Pre-college planning

Junior and senior year are good times to start thinking about:

- **Disability services** at colleges you're applying to. Most have
  experience with food allergies; some are much more equipped than
  others. Ask before you commit.
- **Dining hall investigations.** Visit if you can. Ask the dining
  director about ingredient sourcing, allergen-friendly stations, and
  how they handle student allergies.
- **On-campus medical access.** Where's the health center? What's the
  closest hospital? Does the school health insurance cover allergist
  visits in that town?

See the [college dining guide](/resources/college-dining) for the
practical playbook once you're enrolled.

## When to call your parent

Even after the handover, it's OK to call your parent. Some moments:

- "I just had a reaction. I gave epinephrine. Coming to pick me up?"
- "This restaurant feels off. Should I just leave?"
- "I lost an auto-injector. Need to refill."

The handover doesn't mean you're alone. It means you're driving with
someone in the passenger seat instead of the back.
