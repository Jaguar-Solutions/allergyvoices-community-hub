## Before you go

- **Pick the restaurant carefully.** Restaurants with a written allergy
  policy or a dedicated allergen menu are easier than restaurants without
  one.
- **Call ahead, especially the first time.** Ask whether they can
  accommodate your allergy. The answer (and the way they say it) tells
  you a lot.
- **Avoid the busiest hours** for your first visit. A 5:30 PM weekday
  dinner gives the kitchen more attention than a 7:30 PM Saturday rush.

## The script

When you're seated, lead with this (or your own version):

> "Hi, before we order I want to let you know I have a [peanut / milk /
> sesame] allergy. It's serious — it can cause anaphylaxis. Could you
> please let the manager or chef know so we can talk about what's safe?"

Saying "anaphylaxis" or "serious" matters. Some staff have heard "I'm
allergic to gluten" so often that "allergy" has lost its weight.

When the manager or chef arrives:

> "Thanks for coming over. I'd like to know what dishes you'd recommend
> for someone with [allergen] allergy. I'm asking specifically about
> ingredients **and** about cross-contact — shared fryers, grills,
> or surfaces."

## Questions to ask

For any dish you're considering:

- "Does this dish contain [allergen] in any form?"
- "Are there any [allergen]-containing items prepared on the same
  surface, grill, or fryer?"
- "What's used to thicken the sauce?"
- "What oils are used for cooking and finishing?"
- "Could the chef use a fresh pan and clean utensils for my dish?"

For desserts (often higher-risk):

- "Is the dessert made on-site or brought in?"
- "Are any of your desserts made on shared equipment with [allergen]?"

## Red flags

Walk away (or order plain food only) if:

- The server says "I'm not sure but it should be fine."
- The kitchen seems too busy or chaotic to take the question seriously.
- The chef won't come out, and the server can't answer specifics.
- "Allergy-friendly" claims with no specifics ("we can do that").
- Ingredient lists for sauces or stocks are described as "secret recipe."

You're looking for **specific answers**, not reassurance. "We use peanut
oil in the fryer" is more useful than "you'll be fine."

## Green flags

- The kitchen has a written allergen menu or a printed reference.
- The manager comes to the table without you asking.
- The server says "let me check" rather than guessing.
- They tell you what NOT to order ("the bread has sesame seeds, don't
  order that").
- The chef asks YOU questions to make sure they understand.

## Cross-contact specifics

These come up often enough to mention:

- **Shared fryers** — french fries from a fryer that also cooks shrimp
  tempura are not safe for shellfish allergy. Same for breaded chicken
  in a wheat-cross-contact context, etc.
- **Shared grills** — a chicken breast and a fish fillet on the same
  grill share proteins.
- **Shared woks** — in Asian restaurants, the wok rarely gets washed
  between dishes. Cross-contact is the rule, not the exception.
- **Shared scoops** — ice cream shops, yogurt bars, and bulk-food
  stores are high-cross-contact environments.

## Chef cards

A chef card is a small printed card with your allergy and the rules in
clear language. Hand it to the server and ask them to give it to the
chef.

For travel, translate it. For peanut allergy, for example:

> Spanish: "Tengo alergia al cacahuete. Por favor, no use cacahuetes,
> aceite de cacahuete, o ningún ingrediente que contenga cacahuete. Por
> favor, también evite la contaminación cruzada en la sartén, parrilla, o
> superficies de preparación."

Save the cards on your phone in a notes app for easy access.

## When it goes well

When a restaurant nails it — quiet, careful, attentive — **come back**
and tell them so. Allergy-friendly dining is built on positive
reinforcement. The places that take it seriously deserve repeat business.
