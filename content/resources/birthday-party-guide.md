## When you're attending someone else's party

**Call or text the host parent ahead.** The earlier the better. Most
parents are kind about this and want to get it right.

A short script:

> "Hi! Thanks for inviting [child]. Just wanted to flag that [child] has
> a [peanut / milk / etc.] allergy. We're happy to bring our own [cake /
> snack / etc.] so you don't have to think about it. Could you let me
> know what food and treats you're planning? That way I can pack what we
> need and make sure [child] is set."

This puts you in the driver's seat without putting the host on the spot.

**Pack a "party kit"** to send with your child:

- Two epinephrine auto-injectors and the action plan
- A safe slice of cake or a special-treat alternative (so they have
  something to eat when the cake comes out)
- A safe snack or two
- A water bottle they can use throughout
- A small toy or activity to redirect attention if needed

**Stay if you're not sure.** For younger children, especially before they
fully self-manage, staying at the party (or having one parent stay) is a
reasonable choice. As they get older you'll trust their judgment more.

## When you're hosting

The easier path is **make the entire party allergen-free.** It sounds
restrictive but it's actually freeing &mdash; you don't spend the party
policing what's coming through the door.

For a peanut-free or tree-nut-free party:

- Confirm with the bakery (or bake yourself) that the cake is safe
- Pizza, fruit, vegetables, store-bought safe snacks &mdash; easy to make work
- Goody bags with stickers, small toys, or non-food prizes

If you do allow some allergens (e.g. you can't avoid milk for a milk
allergy), have a designated "safe table" with foods you've personally
verified, and walk your child through which foods are theirs.

## The cake question

Three approaches:

1. **Order the whole cake from a safe bakery.** Most areas have at least
   one allergen-aware bakery. Worth the drive.
2. **Bake at home.** A cake-mix-plus-one-thing approach is usually safe and
   faster than starting from scratch.
3. **Bring a personal cupcake.** When you're attending someone else's
   party, this is the easy out. Wrapped in a clear container, it travels
   well.

## Goody bags

When you're hosting, non-food goody bags solve a lot of problems. Stickers,
small toys, bubbles, mini puzzles &mdash; kids actually love these more than
candy in many cases.

When you're attending, check the goody bag before your child opens it
(in the car is fine). Quietly remove and replace anything that doesn't
work for your allergy.

## When the host gets it wrong

Sometimes you'll arrive at a party where the food isn't safe even though
you communicated ahead. Choices:

- **Stay and use the food you packed.** Your child eats what you brought,
  joins for cake-time with the alternative you brought.
- **Leave early.** It's OK. The friendship survives.
- **Speak up calmly afterward.** "I noticed there were [pretzels with
  peanut butter] out at the party. We were able to handle it, but for next
  time, can you let me know if those are on the menu?"

The goal is teaching your child that food allergies don't keep them out of
celebrations. Most of the time, with planning, that's true.
