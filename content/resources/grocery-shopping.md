## The 10-second label scan

Two places to look on every U.S. food label:

1. **The "Contains:" statement** at the bottom of the ingredient list. By
   law, the top 9 allergens (milk, egg, fish, shellfish, tree nuts,
   peanuts, wheat, soy, sesame) must be declared here in plain language
   if they're in the product.
2. **The ingredient list itself.** Some products only list allergens in
   the ingredient list, not in a separate "Contains:" line. Read both.

If neither contains your allergen, the product is **labeled** safe. (See
"the gray zone" below for what "labeled safe" doesn't always cover.)

## The Top-9 allergens and their alternative names

You don't need to memorize these. The "Contains:" statement does the work.
But if you're reading the ingredient list directly, watch for:

| Allergen | Alternative names to recognize |
| --- | --- |
| **Peanut** | Arachis (rare), peanut flour, peanut butter, peanut oil |
| **Tree nuts** | Each nut listed by name (almond, cashew, walnut...). Marzipan, gianduja, frangipane, nougat, pralines. |
| **Milk** | Casein, caseinate, whey, lactalbumin, lactoglobulin, ghee |
| **Egg** | Albumin, albumen, ovalbumin, ovomucoid, lysozyme |
| **Sesame** | Til, gingelly, benne, tahini, sim sim, gomashio |
| **Wheat** | Durum, semolina, spelt, farro, einkorn, emmer, kamut, bulgur, couscous |
| **Soy** | Soya, soybean, tofu, tempeh, miso, edamame, lecithin (usually soy) |
| **Fish** | Specific fish named (salmon, tuna, anchovy...). Surimi, kamaboko |
| **Shellfish** | Specific shellfish named. Krill, surimi (sometimes shrimp) |

## "May contain" and similar warnings

These warnings are **voluntary, not regulated**:

- "May contain [allergen]"
- "Made on shared equipment with [allergen]"
- "Made in a facility that also processes [allergen]"

Some companies use them broadly out of caution. Others don't use them at
all even when they should. They tell you something but not enough.

**Talk to your allergist about how strict to be on these warnings.** Many
allergists make a recommendation based on your child's specific reaction
history. Some families avoid all "may contain" warnings; others accept
them for low-risk allergens.

## The gray zone

Even with U.S. labeling, a few things are not fully covered:

- **Sesame** — required since January 2023, but some bakeries responded
  by adding sesame to products rather than cleaning lines. Recheck old
  trusted brands.
- **Mollusks** (clam, oyster, mussel, scallop) are NOT covered by FALCPA.
  They may appear under generic terms like "seafood flavoring."
- **Highly refined oils** (peanut, soybean) are exempt from labeling.
  Most are tolerated by allergy patients, but check with your allergist.
- **Cross-contact during processing** isn't required to be declared.
- **Restaurants and bakeries** aren't covered by FALCPA at all. The
  in-store muffin or salad bar item often doesn't have a label.

## When to email or call the manufacturer

- The label is ambiguous (a "natural flavor" you can't trace)
- You want to know about cross-contact in the facility
- A formulation seems to have changed

Most companies have an allergen email address or a toll-free number.
Responses sometimes take a few days but are usually clear when they
arrive.

## What changes a "trusted" product

- **New owner or co-packer.** Companies acquire each other; manufacturing
  moves. The label can change subtly.
- **A reformulation.** Watch for "new and improved" callouts — that's a
  cue to recheck the label.
- **A new product line.** A brand might add a peanut-containing variant
  to its lineup. Cross-contact risk goes up across the line.

A good habit: **read every label every time, even for products you buy
weekly.** It takes 10 seconds. Once or twice a year, that 10 seconds
catches a change that matters.

## Building a safe-products list

Keep a running note in your phone with brand-and-product names you've
verified. Group by category (cookies, breads, sauces, etc.) for fast
shopping. Recheck once a year.

This list is easier to maintain than memorizing every ingredient. It also
makes meal-planning faster — you start from "what we know works" rather
than from a blank slate.
