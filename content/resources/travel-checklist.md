## Before you go

- [ ] **Auto-injectors not expiring during the trip** &mdash; refill if needed
- [ ] **Antihistamines** packed in their original bottle
- [ ] **Emergency action plan** (paper copy and photo on phone)
- [ ] **Doctor's letter** for international travel explaining the
  medications &mdash; useful at customs and for purchasing replacements
  abroad if needed
- [ ] **Chef card** in the local language(s) of your destination
  (translation services exist; ask your allergist or use a vetted
  template)
- [ ] **Travel insurance** that covers medical evacuation, especially
  international
- [ ] **Locate hospitals** at your destination &mdash; bookmark addresses on
  your phone
- [ ] **Safe snacks** for travel days: granola bars, fruit, crackers, etc.

## At the airport (USA)

- **Auto-injectors travel in carry-on** with you, not checked. Cargo
  holds get cold and aren't accessible during a reaction.
- **TSA allows epinephrine auto-injectors** without the usual liquid
  limits. Keep them in their original pharmacy packaging.
- **Mention them to TSA** at the start of the X-ray screening: "I have
  prescription epinephrine in my bag." They may need to inspect.
- **Antihistamines and medications** also stay in carry-on, original
  bottles.

## On the plane

- **Don't put auto-injectors in the overhead bin.** Seat pocket or your
  bag at your feet, not 30 seconds away.
- **Pre-board if you have a peanut/tree-nut allergy** to wipe down your
  seat area. Most airlines accommodate this if you ask in advance.
- **Don't rely on airline meals** &mdash; even "allergy-friendly" options vary
  in reliability. Pack what you'll eat.
- **Tell the flight attendants** about the allergy when you board. They
  can sometimes adjust nearby snacks (some airlines stop peanut snack
  service if a peanut-allergic passenger is on board).

## At the hotel

- **Request a room with a kitchenette** if available. Even a microwave
  and mini-fridge gives you breakfast options.
- **Stock the fridge on day one.** A nearby grocery store with safe
  staples removes pressure from every meal decision.
- **Check that the room has a working thermostat** if you're storing
  auto-injectors at room temperature.
- **Locate the nearest hospital** before you need it.

## Eating out abroad

- **Lead with the chef card.** Hand it to the server; ask them to take
  it to the kitchen and bring it back when the chef has read it.
- **Watch for cross-contact** in cuisines that heavily use your
  allergen. Asian cuisines often use peanut, sesame, soy, fish sauce.
  Mediterranean cuisines often use sesame and tree nuts.
- **When in doubt, eat at the hotel** or grab packaged food from a
  grocery store with clear labels.
- **The first dinner of any trip is the most stressful.** Pick a
  research-vetted restaurant for it; everything else gets easier from
  there.

## International specifics

- **Country labeling laws differ.** EU labeling covers 14 allergens; US
  covers 9. Some countries don't require allergen declarations at all.
- **Carry a doctor's letter** in English and, ideally, the destination's
  language.
- **Know the local emergency number** (911 in US, 112 in much of Europe,
  119 in Japan, 000 in Australia, etc.). Save it in your phone before
  you fly.
- **Replacement auto-injectors abroad** &mdash; brand and dosing differ from
  country to country. Try not to need this; if you do, the doctor's letter
  helps. EpiPen is widely available; Auvi-Q is not.

## After you're home

- Note what worked and what didn't in your phone (or this guide). The
  next trip will be easier.
- Replace any auto-injector you used in transit (and call 911 if you
  used one &mdash; observation in a hospital still applies, even abroad).
