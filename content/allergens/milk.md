## The basics

Cow's milk allergy is the most common food allergy in infants and young
children, affecting roughly 2-3% of children under three. The body's immune
system reacts to one or more milk proteins &mdash; most often **casein** and
**whey**.

A few things milk allergy is **not**:

- **Lactose intolerance.** That's a digestive issue (the body can't break
  down milk sugar). It causes bloating and discomfort, not anaphylaxis.
- **FPIES (food protein-induced enterocolitis).** Different mechanism
  (delayed, GI-driven), different management. Your allergist can
  distinguish.

The good news: many children outgrow cow's milk allergy by school age,
especially if their reaction history is mild and their numbers improve over
time. Some carry it into adulthood.

## Reading labels

In the United States, FALCPA requires "milk" to be declared in plain
language &mdash; either in the ingredient list or in a "Contains: milk" line.
That covers casein, whey, lactalbumin, lactoglobulin, and other derivatives.

Common milk-derived ingredients to recognize:

- **Casein, caseinate, sodium caseinate, calcium caseinate**
- **Whey, whey protein concentrate, whey protein isolate**
- **Lactalbumin, lactoglobulin**
- **Ghee** (clarified butter &mdash; still contains some milk proteins)
- **Butter, butter fat, buttermilk**
- **Milk solids, milk powder, dry milk**

What the rules don't cover well:

- **"Non-dairy"** is a marketing term, not a legal one. Non-dairy creamers,
  whipped toppings, and cheese substitutes are sometimes made with
  caseinate. Always read the ingredient list.
- **Cross-contact** at deli counters and ice cream shops is common because
  shared equipment isn't covered by labeling rules.

## Hidden sources

Milk shows up in places that don't seem dairy at all:

- **Deli meats** sliced on the same blade as cheese
- **Hot dogs, sausages, and chicken nuggets** (some recipes use casein as
  a binder)
- **Tuna** (a small number of brands add hydrolyzed casein)
- **Brown sugar, marshmallow, marzipan** (varies by brand)
- **Probiotics and some medications** (the bacteria are often cultured on
  milk-based media, leaving traces)
- **Dark chocolate** (often produced on shared equipment with milk chocolate)
- **Some artificial flavors and "natural flavors"** &mdash; if the label
  doesn't say milk-free explicitly, ask the manufacturer.

## Daily life

**At home:** Most families with milk-allergic infants choose to keep the
home milk-free initially. As the child grows older and confident, some
households reintroduce milk products in clearly separated areas.

**Formula:** For milk-allergic infants, allergists typically recommend
extensively hydrolyzed or amino-acid-based formulas, **not** soy formula
unless specifically directed.

**The baked-milk ladder:** Many milk-allergic children tolerate milk that
has been baked into a muffin or cookie at high temperature for long enough.
This is sometimes used as a step toward outgrowing the allergy. **It
requires medical supervision** &mdash; do not try this at home without your
allergist.

**At school:** Coordinate with the school nurse, cafeteria, and teacher.
Field trips, birthday treats, and food-based crafts (yogurt cups, ice cream
parties) all need a plan.

**Eating out:** Italian, Mexican, and bakery-heavy menus tend to use a lot
of milk. Asian and many vegan-leaning restaurants tend to use less. Always
ask &mdash; "vegan" usually means milk-free, but not always.

## Treatments

Milk **oral immunotherapy** (OIT) is offered at some specialty allergy
centers. The protocol typically goes hand-in-hand with the baked-milk
ladder. **Omalizumab** (Xolair) was approved in 2024 to reduce reactions
from accidental exposure across multiple foods, including milk.

Like all food allergy treatments, these are personal decisions weighed
against time commitment, side effects, and family readiness.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- What's our current likelihood of outgrowing this allergy?
- Are we candidates for the baked-milk ladder?
- Are we candidates for milk OIT or omalizumab?
- Are goat or sheep milk safe? (Almost always: no.)
- What's our threshold for using epinephrine after accidental exposure?
- Do I need to avoid medications and probiotics that might contain trace
  milk?
