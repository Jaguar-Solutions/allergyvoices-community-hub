## The basics

"Shellfish" is a category that covers two biologically distinct groups:

- **Crustaceans:** shrimp, crab, lobster, prawn, crayfish, langoustine
- **Mollusks:** clam, oyster, scallop, mussel, octopus, squid, snail

The main allergenic protein in crustaceans is **tropomyosin**. Mollusk
allergies are less common and often have different proteins. Many
shellfish-allergic patients react across both groups; others tolerate
mollusks while reacting to crustaceans (or the reverse). Your allergist
can test individually.

Shellfish is the **most common adult-onset food allergy** in the U.S. It
tends to develop in adolescence or adulthood and is usually lifelong.

## Common myths to clear up

- **Iodine.** Shellfish allergy is **not** an iodine allergy. The
  allergenic protein has nothing to do with iodine. CT-scan contrast dyes
  containing iodine are safe for shellfish-allergic patients (the AAAAI
  has confirmed this for years).
- **Cooking deactivates it.** Tropomyosin is heat-stable. Cooked shellfish
  is just as allergenic as raw.
- **Vapor exposure.** Some highly allergic patients react to airborne
  shellfish proteins from cooking steam. This is real and worth knowing
  about, especially in seafood restaurants and on cruises.

## Reading labels

FALCPA requires the **specific crustacean** to be named — "shrimp,"
"crab," "lobster," etc. **Mollusks are not covered by FALCPA**, which is
worth knowing. They may be listed under generic terms like "seafood" or
"shellfish flavoring."

Watch for:

- **Shrimp, crab, lobster, prawn, crayfish** (FALCPA-covered)
- **Krill** (often in supplements and fish-oil substitutes)
- **Clam, oyster, mussel, scallop, octopus, squid, calamari** (mollusks,
  not FALCPA-covered)
- **Glucosamine** — usually shellfish-derived; vegan glucosamine exists
- **Oyster-shell calcium** in some supplements
- **Surimi** — often crab-flavored, sometimes shrimp-based

## Hidden sources

- **Caesar dressing** — some Asian-style versions include shrimp paste
- **Asian fish sauces and shrimp pastes** — nuoc mam, terasi, belacan
- **Worcestershire sauce variants** in Asia (some include shrimp)
- **Paella, jambalaya, gumbo, bouillabaisse**
- **Surimi, imitation crab, California rolls**
- **Some Caesar pizzas, seafood pizzas**
- **Glucosamine and chondroitin supplements**
- **Cross-contact** in shared fryers (a fryer used for shrimp tempura is
  not safe for fries)

## Daily life

**At home:** Many shellfish-allergic households simply don't keep
shellfish at all because cross-contact in a shared kitchen is hard to
manage.

**At school:** Less of an issue than peanut or milk because shellfish is
rare in school cafeterias. Watch field trips and special events.

**Eating out:** Seafood restaurants, sushi restaurants, and Asian
restaurants are highest-risk. Many shellfish-allergic patients avoid
them entirely. **Cross-contact in shared fryers, grills, and woks** is the
most common cause of accidental exposure — ask specifically.

**Travel:** Coastal regions and many Asian cuisines use shellfish heavily.
Pack a chef card and review menus carefully.

**Vapor exposure:** If you're highly sensitive, seafood markets, busy
seafood restaurants, and certain workplaces (commercial kitchens, fish
processing) can trigger reactions. Talk to your allergist about whether
this applies to you.

## Treatments

There is no widely available oral immunotherapy for shellfish.
**Omalizumab** (Xolair) was approved in 2024 for multiple food allergies;
ask your allergist whether your shellfish allergy fits the criteria.

For most shellfish-allergic patients, the management plan is **strict
avoidance, two epinephrine auto-injectors, and an emergency action plan**.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- Which specific shellfish do I need to avoid? Crustaceans only, or
  mollusks too?
- Am I at risk for vapor reactions in seafood-heavy environments?
- Am I a candidate for omalizumab?
- Is glucosamine safe for me, or should I look for a vegan source?
- What's my threshold for using epinephrine after accidental exposure?
