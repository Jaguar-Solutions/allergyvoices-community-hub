## The basics

Wheat allergy is an IgE-mediated reaction to wheat proteins, most commonly
**gliadin**, **glutenin**, and **albumin**. It can cause anaphylaxis,
unlike celiac disease (which is autoimmune) or non-celiac gluten
sensitivity (which is poorly understood and not life-threatening).

These three things are often confused but require different management:

| Condition | Mechanism | Management | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wheat allergy** | IgE-mediated allergy | Avoid wheat; carry epinephrine | Can be life-threatening |
| **Celiac disease** | Autoimmune | Avoid all gluten (wheat, barley, rye) | Long-term gut damage if untreated |
| **Non-celiac gluten sensitivity** | Unclear | Avoid gluten if it makes you feel better | Generally not dangerous |

If your child has wheat allergy, gluten-free products are usually safe to
eat &mdash; but you don't need to avoid barley, rye, or oats unless they're
contaminated.

Many children outgrow wheat allergy, often by ages 5-8. Track tolerance
with your allergist over time.

## Reading labels

FALCPA requires "wheat" to be declared in plain language. Look for these
related ingredients:

- **Wheat, wheat flour, whole wheat, durum, semolina, spelt, farro,
  einkorn, emmer, kamut, triticale**
- **Bulgur, couscous**
- **Wheat starch, wheat germ, wheat bran**
- **Hydrolyzed wheat protein, wheat gluten**

Be cautious with these "may contain" candidates:

- **Modified food starch** &mdash; in U.S. labeling, must declare wheat if
  derived from wheat
- **Hydrolyzed vegetable protein** &mdash; same
- **Natural flavoring** &mdash; ask the manufacturer

## Hidden sources

- **Soy sauce** &mdash; most contains wheat; **tamari** is the wheat-free version
- **Imitation crab and surimi** &mdash; wheat is a common binder
- **Hot dogs, sausages, deli meats** &mdash; wheat as filler
- **Beer** &mdash; most beer is brewed from wheat or barley
- **Communion wafers** &mdash; some churches now offer rice or low-gluten options
- **Play-doh** &mdash; the popular brand contains wheat; if your child has
  contact reactions, look for wheat-free alternatives
- **Some medications** &mdash; wheat starch is occasionally used as a binder

## Daily life

**At home:** Wheat-free baking is more accessible than ever, with rice,
corn, oat, almond, and tapioca flours readily available. Many wheat-free
families overlap their pantry with gluten-free products.

**At school:** Cafeteria menus often have hidden wheat (breaded chicken,
pasta, soy-sauce-based stir fries). Coordinate with the school nutrition
team. Ask about backup options on bagel days and pizza days.

**Eating out:** Italian, bakery-heavy, and Asian restaurants are
highest-risk. The growth of gluten-free menus has made wheat-free dining
much easier. Ask the kitchen to confirm a "gluten-free" item is also
prepared without cross-contact &mdash; gluten-free preparation usually meets
wheat-free needs.

## Treatments

There is no FDA-approved wheat OIT product. Some specialty allergy centers
offer **investigational wheat OIT**. **Omalizumab** (Xolair) was approved
in 2024 for multiple food allergies including wheat, raising the threshold
for accidental-exposure reactions.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- Is my child likely to outgrow this? What do their numbers suggest?
- Are we candidates for wheat OIT or omalizumab?
- Is the gluten-free section at our grocery store reliable for wheat
  avoidance?
- What's the threshold for using epinephrine?
- Should we avoid oats? (Often safe; sometimes cross-contaminated.)
