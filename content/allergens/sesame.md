## The basics

Sesame allergy was officially recognized as a major U.S. food allergen
when the FASTER Act took effect on January 1, 2023, requiring "sesame" to
be declared on labels in plain language. Before that, it could appear under
many names with no warning.

Sesame allergy has been growing in U.S. children, with prevalence estimates
around 0.2-0.6%. Reactions can be severe; sesame is one of the more common
triggers of anaphylaxis in children outside the older Top-8 allergens.

It's important to know the alternative names: **tahini, til, gingelly,
benne, sim sim, gomashio**. They're all sesame.

## Reading labels

Since January 2023, U.S. food labels must declare **"sesame"** in plain
language — either in the ingredient list or in a "Contains:" statement.

What the rules don't cover well:

- **Cross-contact** during manufacturing. "May contain sesame" is voluntary
  and inconsistent.
- **Restaurants and bakeries.** Federal labeling doesn't apply to most
  restaurant menus.
- **The FASTER Act loophole.** Some bakeries responded to the new rule by
  intentionally adding sesame flour to their products (so they could
  declare it once on the label) rather than cleaning their lines. If a
  product you used to trust changed after January 2023, recheck.

## Hidden sources

Sesame is widespread in:

- **Middle Eastern cuisine** — hummus, baba ghanoush, tahini-based sauces,
  falafel, halvah
- **Asian cuisine** — sesame oil, sesame seeds on noodles, dumpling
  fillings, certain dressings
- **Bakery items** — multigrain breads, "everything" bagels, breadsticks,
  buns with seeded tops
- **Granola, trail mix, energy bars**
- **Salad dressings and marinades** (tahini and sesame oil)
- **Vegetarian patties and meat substitutes**
- **Some processed snacks and chips**

## Daily life

**At home:** Read every label on items purchased after January 2023, and
recheck older trusted brands. Tahini and sesame oil hide in many sauces.

**At school:** Sesame is less likely to appear in classroom snacks than
peanut or milk, but it shows up on bagel days and in bread-based meals.
Coordinate with the cafeteria.

**Eating out:** Middle Eastern, Mediterranean, and Asian restaurants are
highest-risk. Even non-sesame menu items can be cross-contacted from
shared sauces and grills. Ask directly.

**Travel:** Sesame is heavily used in Middle Eastern, Mediterranean,
Indian, and East Asian cuisines. Pack a chef card translated for your
destination.

## Treatments

**Omalizumab** (Xolair) was approved in 2024 to reduce reactions from
accidental exposure to multiple foods, including sesame. **Sesame OIT**
is offered at some specialty allergy centers, though the evidence base is
smaller than for peanut.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- Is my child a candidate for omalizumab? For sesame OIT?
- Is highly refined sesame oil safe for us, or should we avoid all forms?
- What's our threshold for using epinephrine after accidental exposure?
- Are there sesame-related products I should be re-checking after the
  FASTER Act took effect?
