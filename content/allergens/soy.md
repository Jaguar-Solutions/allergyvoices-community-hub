## The basics

Soy allergy affects about 0.4% of U.S. children. Many outgrow it by school
age. Reactions are usually mild but can be severe, so soy is treated as a
top allergen and gets the same labeling protections.

Two practical points families often hear:

- **Highly refined soybean oil** is exempt from FALCPA labeling because
  the refining process removes nearly all soy protein. Most soy-allergic
  patients tolerate it, but always confirm with your allergist.
- **Soy lecithin** is similarly highly processed and usually tolerated,
  but it does contain trace soy protein. Whether to avoid it depends on
  your reaction history; ask your allergist.

## Reading labels

FALCPA requires "soy" to be declared in plain language. Watch for:

- **Soy, soybean, soya, soy protein, soy protein isolate, soy protein
  concentrate**
- **Tofu, tempeh, edamame, miso, natto**
- **Hydrolyzed soy protein, textured vegetable protein (TVP)**
- **Soy sauce, tamari, shoyu**
- **Lecithin** &mdash; usually soy-derived (sunflower lecithin is the safer
  alternative)

What the rules don't cover well:

- **Highly refined soybean oil** &mdash; legally not required to be declared,
  but most labels do mention it now
- **Cross-contact** during manufacturing in shared lines

## Hidden sources

Soy is one of the most-widely-used food ingredients in U.S. processed food:

- **Most Asian sauces** &mdash; soy sauce, teriyaki, hoisin, oyster sauce, fish
  sauce blends, miso
- **Vegetable broth and bouillon cubes** (read every label)
- **Vegetarian meat substitutes** &mdash; veggie burgers, "chicken" nuggets,
  hot dogs, faux deli slices
- **Energy bars and protein bars**
- **Many baked goods** (soy flour, soy lecithin)
- **Most infant formulas** (talk to your pediatrician about hydrolyzed or
  amino-acid alternatives if your infant is soy-allergic)
- **Some chocolate** (soy lecithin as emulsifier)

## Daily life

**At home:** Soy-free cooking gets easier once you know the substitutes:
**coconut aminos** for soy sauce, **sunflower or pea-based** veggie burgers,
**rice or oat milk** if also avoiding milk.

**At school:** Cafeteria menus often have soy in unexpected places (chicken
nuggets, soy-glazed proteins, bread). Coordinate with the kitchen.

**Eating out:** Asian restaurants are highest-risk because soy is
foundational to most cuisines. Vegetarian/vegan menus often rely heavily
on soy too. Ask about every sauce and marinade.

**Travel:** Asian and South American cuisines use soy extensively. Pack
a chef card.

## Treatments

**Soy OIT** is offered at some specialty allergy centers, though the
evidence base is smaller than for peanut. **Omalizumab** (Xolair) was
approved in 2024 for multiple food allergies including soy.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- Is highly refined soybean oil safe for us? What about soy lecithin?
- Is my child likely to outgrow this?
- Are we candidates for soy OIT or omalizumab?
- What's the threshold for using epinephrine after accidental exposure?
- Are there soy-derived ingredients in any current medications I should
  watch for?
