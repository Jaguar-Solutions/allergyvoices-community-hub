## The basics

Peanuts grow underground (they're a legume, like beans and lentils), so peanut
allergy is biologically separate from tree nut allergies like almond or cashew.
That said, many people allergic to peanut are also allergic to one or more tree
nuts &mdash; about 25-40% by some estimates. Your allergist can test for both.

Peanut allergy tends to start in early childhood and is more often lifelong than
milk or egg allergy. Recent guidelines support **early introduction** of
peanut-containing foods to lower the risk of developing a peanut allergy in
infants who don't already have one &mdash; talk to your pediatrician about
timing, especially if your baby has eczema or an egg allergy.

## Reading labels

In the United States, FALCPA (and the FASTER Act for sesame) requires plain-language
allergen labeling. For peanut, that means the word **"peanut"** has to appear
either in the ingredient list or in a "Contains: peanut" statement.

What labels **don't** cover well:

- **Cross-contact** during manufacturing. "May contain peanut," "made on shared
  equipment," or "made in a facility that processes peanut" are voluntary and
  inconsistent. Some brands use them broadly; others don't use them at all.
- **Restaurants** and bakeries. Federal labeling rules don't apply to most
  restaurant menus. Ask about cross-contact directly.

## Hidden sources

Peanut shows up in unexpected places &mdash; especially in cuisines that use it as
a thickener or sauce base. Always ask about ingredients in:

- Mole, satay sauce, and pad thai
- Vegetarian meat substitutes (peanut flour is a common protein source)
- Granola bars, energy bars, trail mix
- Bakery cookies, brownies, and muffins (shared equipment)
- Some chili recipes (peanut butter is sometimes used to thicken)

## Daily life

**At home:** Decide on a household policy that works for everyone. Some
families go fully peanut-free at home; others keep peanut products in clearly
labeled, separated areas.

**At school:** Ask the school nurse and teacher specifically what
"peanut-free" means in their building. Does it apply only to the classroom?
The cafeteria? Snacks brought from home? Birthday treats? Field trips?

**Eating out:** Speak directly to the manager or chef. Ask about shared
fryers, woks, and prep surfaces. Asian, African, and Mexican kitchens often
use peanut as a regular ingredient &mdash; ask before assuming a dish is safe.

**Travel:** Pack two epinephrine auto-injectors (always two), antihistamines,
a chef card in the local language, and safe snacks for the journey.

## Treatments

Peanut is the only food allergen with an FDA-approved oral immunotherapy
(OIT) product (**Palforzia**), approved for children ages 4-17. Other OIT and
sublingual immunotherapy approaches are offered at specialty allergy centers.
**Omalizumab** (Xolair) was approved by the FDA in 2024 to reduce the risk of
allergic reactions to multiple foods, including peanut, after accidental
exposure &mdash; it doesn't cure allergy but raises the threshold.

These are personal decisions. Talk through risks, time commitment, and your
child's readiness with an allergist who specializes in food allergy.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- What's the latest blood test or skin test number for my child, and how has
  it changed over time?
- Is my child a candidate for OIT? For omalizumab?
- What thresholds are in our action plan, and when should we use epinephrine
  versus call you?
- Are there situations where you'd recommend a hospital stay for accidental
  exposure?
- What's the most current advice on cross-contact labeling?
