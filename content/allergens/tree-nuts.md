## The basics

"Tree nuts" is an umbrella label for about a dozen distinct foods: almond,
cashew, walnut, pecan, hazelnut, pistachio, Brazil nut, macadamia, pine nut,
and a handful of less-common ones. They look related and they share a label
category, but they're botanically diverse &mdash; some are seeds of fruit (almond,
cashew, pistachio), some are true nuts (hazelnut, chestnut), and some are
something else entirely.

This matters because **being allergic to one tree nut doesn't mean you're
allergic to all of them**. Many people react to cashew and pistachio
together (they're related) but tolerate almond. Others react to walnut and
pecan but not cashew. Your allergist can test for individual tree nuts and
help you build the list that fits your situation.

About 25-40% of people with peanut allergy also have a tree nut allergy &mdash;
worth knowing, but the two are biologically distinct.

## Reading labels

In the United States, FALCPA requires the **specific tree nut** to be named
on the label &mdash; "almond," "cashew," "walnut," etc. &mdash; rather than just
"tree nuts."

What the rules don't cover well:

- **"Made on shared equipment with tree nuts."** Voluntary, inconsistent,
  and not regulated. Some bakeries use it heavily; others not at all.
- **Restaurants and bakeries.** Cross-contact is common, especially in
  bakeries and ice cream shops where scoops, mixers, and surfaces are shared.

## Hidden sources

Tree nuts hide most often in baked goods, sauces, and ethnic cuisines. Always
ask about ingredients in:

- Pesto (pine nuts; sometimes walnuts)
- Mole, romesco, and certain curry pastes
- Baklava, gianduja, marzipan
- Trail mix, granola, and energy bars
- Vegetarian meat substitutes (cashew is a common protein base)
- Some pâtés and stuffings
- Liqueurs like amaretto and Frangelico

## Daily life

**At home:** Decide on a household policy that fits your specific allergy
list. Some families remove all tree nuts; others keep tolerated ones in
clearly labeled, separated areas.

**At school:** Ask the school what "nut-free" means in their building &mdash;
specifically whether it covers both peanuts and tree nuts and whether it
applies to classroom snacks, the cafeteria, and outside food at events.

**Eating out:** Bakeries, ice cream shops, and Asian restaurants are
high-cross-contact environments. Speak directly to the manager or chef and
ask about shared scoops, prep surfaces, and grills.

**Travel:** Pack two epinephrine auto-injectors, a chef card, and safe
snacks. International labeling rules vary &mdash; some countries don't
require specific tree nut naming.

## Treatments

There is no FDA-approved oral immunotherapy product for tree nuts (Palforzia
is peanut-only as of this writing). Some specialty centers offer
**individual** OIT for cashew, walnut, almond, etc.

**Omalizumab** (Xolair) was approved in 2024 to reduce reactions from
accidental exposure to multiple foods, including tree nuts. It doesn't cure
allergy but raises the threshold. Talk to an allergist who specializes in
food allergy if you want to weigh OIT or omalizumab.

## Questions to ask your allergist

- Which specific tree nuts does my child react to, and how have those
  numbers changed over time?
- Is coconut safe for us, or should we avoid it too?
- Are we candidates for individual-tree-nut OIT, or for omalizumab?
- What's the action plan threshold for accidental exposure?
- Do I need to avoid tree nut oils and butters, or is highly refined oil OK?
